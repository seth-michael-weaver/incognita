    Inside a Nuclear Reaction

    One neutron. One nucleus. Many ways for it to end.

    The INCOGNITA engine is a port of TALYS-2 (A. J. Koning, S. Hilaire, S. Goriely) and uses the TALYS nuclear-structure database. What INCOGNITA adds is listed, switch by switch, at the end of this page.

    Scroll to begin ↓

    Fire a neutron at an atomic nucleus and something comes back out. It might be the neutron itself, bounced off. It might be a proton, an alpha particle (a tight cluster of 2 protons and 2 neutrons), or a burst of light. A heavy nucleus might even split in two.

    Which one happens, and how often? This page walks through how our software answers that question, one step at a time. Scroll down, and try the controls along the way. The pictures follow the same physics as the engine; the numbers in them are illustrative.

    Who it is for: anyone curious. No physics needed for the first chapters; later ones go to research level. Each chapter carries a tag: Intro, Intermediate or Advanced. Teaching with it? See the guide for instructors.

    Where this lives in the code

    Each stage above is a package in physics/hf, and the engine calls them in the order you just read. The engine is a port of TALYS-2.x, the established reaction code. What it adds sits behind named switches inside the same packages: most are off by default, a few corrections are on, and each one can be switched back to plain TALYS on its own.

    Outside the engine

    Some parts of the story run beside the engine rather than inside it. The statistical resonance ladders of Chapter 10 live in physics/resonance/. The Maxwellian averages (MACS) of Chapter 11 are folded from the engine's capture curves after the run, in validation/differential/core.py; capture on thermally excited targets and the stellar rate, by contrast, come from the engine itself (astro/stellar.py, optional). The anchoring layer of Chapter 12 lives in incognita/terra/.

    The code, the library and the curated measurements are open: seth-michael-weaver/incognita on GitHub. You can look up any prediction yourself on the Every nuclide page, or clone the repository and run it.
