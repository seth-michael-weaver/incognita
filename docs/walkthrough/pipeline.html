How nuclear data reach a reactor calculation

The stages above are the same steps that every reactor and safety code depends on, used the world over. Here they are in more detail: who makes the data, how the files are organised, what the processing does, how a Monte Carlo code uses the result, and where uncertainty enters.

Who makes the evaluations

An evaluation is a complete, consistent set of data for one nuclide: every reaction, at every energy from a hundred-thousandth of an electronvolt to 20 MeV or more, chosen by evaluators who weigh the measurements against nuclear models. National and international projects publish whole libraries of them: ENDF/B in the United States, JEFF in Europe, JENDL in Japan, CENDL in China, BROND in Russia, and TENDL, built largely automatically with the TALYS reaction code. A new release takes years and is tested against criticality experiments before it is adopted.

The ENDF-6 format at a glance

All of these libraries are written in ENDF-6, a fixed-column text format from the 1960s. Each nuclide is a material (MAT), each kind of data a file (MF), and each reaction a section (MT). Stage 3 above shows how one file is laid out.

At low energy a file usually stores resonance parameters, a few numbers per resonance, from which the cross section is computed with an R-matrix formula (From resonances to averages). At higher energy it stores the cross section itself, as a table of energies and values.

NJOY, module by module

Transport codes cannot read ENDF-6 directly. NJOY, from Los Alamos, is the standard processing system: each module does one job, and a run chains them.

  • RECONRTurns resonance parameters into a table of cross sections, on an energy grid fine enough that straight lines between points stay within a set tolerance. Transport codes look up tables, not formulas.
  • BROADRDoppler-broadens the tables to the temperatures of fuel and moderator. Hot nuclei move, which smears the peaks and gives a reactor its temperature feedback (Doppler broadening; stage 4 above).
  • UNRESR · PURRIn the unresolved range, builds self-shielded averages (UNRESR) or probability tables (PURR, for Monte Carlo) from randomly sampled resonance ladders. Those resonances are too close to measure one by one but still shadow each other.
  • THERMRAdds thermal scattering: a free gas, or scattering laws for hydrogen in water, graphite and other moderators, where a slow neutron feels the chemical binding of the atom it hits.
  • HEATR · GASPRCompute the heat each reaction deposits, the damage to the material, and the hydrogen and helium gas it produces: they set temperatures, swelling and embrittlement.
  • ERRORR · COVRTurn the covariances into uncertainty matrices on energy groups, ready to be carried through to a result.
  • ACER · GROUPRACER writes the ACE tables read by Monte Carlo codes such as MCNP, Serpent and OpenMC (OpenMC converts them to its HDF5 library). GROUPR averages over energy groups for the faster deterministic codes.

Processing one nuclide at a handful of temperatures takes seconds for a light nucleus and minutes for a heavy one with thousands of resonances; a full library of several hundred nuclides is a job of hours.

OpenMC: from library to k-eff

OpenMC is an open-source Monte Carlo transport code. A cross_sections.xml index points it to one HDF5 file per nuclide and per scattering material; it interpolates between the stored temperatures and samples the probability tables in the unresolved range. It then follows neutrons one at a time through a three-dimensional model, drawing each flight distance, collision, and outgoing energy and angle from the library (Neutron transport and Monte Carlo).

To find k-eff it runs in batches: the fission sites of one generation start the next, and k-eff is the ratio of neutrons from one generation to the next, averaged once the source has settled. Tallies record reaction rates, flux and heating anywhere in the model. The statistical error falls as one over the square root of the number of neutrons, so halving it costs four times as long: a simple critical experiment takes minutes, a detailed reactor core hours on many processors.

Where uncertainty comes in

Every evaluated number is uncertain, and evaluations publish covariances that say how much, and how the errors of different energies and reactions move together. Two standard routes carry them to a result. Sensitivity: compute how much k-eff changes for a small change in each cross section, then combine these with the covariances. Sampling: draw many random versions of the data, or of the model parameters behind them, process and run each one, and read the spread of the answers. Sampling is simple and general but costs hundreds of full calculations, which is where a fast emulator helps.

Work we are developing

Light nuclei: resonances all the way up

In a heavy nucleus such as uranium a neutron's energy is shared among a vast number of compound levels, and above a few keV the cross section is smooth enough for statistical theory (Hauser–Feshbach). Light nuclei, from hydrogen to oxygen, have only a handful of widely spaced levels. Their cross sections are shaped by a few broad resonances up to MeV energies, and statistical theory never applies.

These few nuclei matter out of all proportion. Hydrogen, deuterium, carbon and oxygen slow the neutrons in reactors. Lithium-6 and boron-10 absorb them in control rods, shielding and detectors, and their (n,t) and (n,α) reactions, with neutron–proton scattering, are the international standards against which other measurements are calibrated. Nitrogen-14(n,p) makes carbon-14 in air and coolant; oxygen-16(n,α) makes helium in water. Below their resonances, absorption falls as one over the neutron's speed (What is a cross section?).

The tool is R-matrix theory. Space is split at a channel radius: inside, the nuclear force acts and is described by a set of levels with energies and widths; outside, only the known Coulomb and centrifugal forces act, with one channel for each pair of particles that can come out. Fitting the levels to every measured reaction at once, elastic, capture, (n,α), (n,p) and their angular distributions, gives cross sections in which the probabilities of all channels add up correctly (unitarity). Hydrogen itself has no resonances to fit: neutron–proton scattering is set directly by the force between two nucleons.

We are developing multi-channel R-matrix fits for light nuclei that use the measurements through a model of each experiment, as in stage 2 above.

Resonances, resolved and unresolved

A resonance is one level of the compound nucleus: when the neutron's energy matches it, the cross section jumps by orders of magnitude. An isolated level has the Breit–Wigner shape; when levels interfere, evaluations use the Reich–Moore form of R-matrix theory. The physics chapters explain both step by step: Resonances and From resonances to averages.

At low energy each resonance is measured on its own, by time of flight, and its parameters are fitted: the resolved region. Higher up the resonances blur together within the instruments' resolution. Only their averages are known, the mean spacing, average widths and strength functions, and statistical ladders sampled from them stand in: the unresolved region. Self-shielding still matters there, so processing turns those ladders into probability tables (PURR above), and temperature smears everything through Doppler broadening.

We are developing resonance evaluations fitted to raw measurements through a model of each experiment, and statistical ladders that carry their uncertainty into the unresolved range.

An approach we are developing

Emulators: a fast stand-in for the physics model

A nuclear reaction model takes seconds to minutes to compute one nucleus at one setting of its physics parameters. An emulator (or surrogate) is a second, much simpler model trained on many runs of the first: it learns how the outputs, the cross sections, change as the parameters change, and then answers in milliseconds.

Speed matters because the most useful questions need thousands of runs, not one: uncertainty (sample the parameters within what the measurements allow and watch the spread of the result), sensitivity (which parameter moves which reaction, and where in energy), and fitting (search for the parameters that best match the measurements). An emulator is only trusted where it was trained, and it is checked against the full model on runs it never saw.

The two shortcuts attack the two slow steps. The first replaces model, file, processing by one learned map from physics parameters to the library a transport code reads. The second replaces Monte Carlo, which follows millions of individual neutrons, by a deterministic calculation that solves for the average neutron population directly, much faster for simple systems though less general. This is an approach we are developing; it is not part of the released files.
